# High Haematocrit on TRT — Is It Harmful, or Just Better Oxygen Transport?

**Compiled:** 2026-06-26 · evidence-graded research review
**Question:** For a 41M on TRT at Hct 0.51 (with OSA, HTN, recurrent epistaxis, tadalafil), is deferring phlebotomy/donation for ~3 months of CPAP + Zone 2 reasonable — and is the "high Hct = superior oxygen transport, not dangerous viscosity" argument valid?

---

## Bottom line (read this first)

1. **Deferring phlebotomy is well-supported — arguably the evidence-preferred move.** There is *no* direct evidence that phlebotomy reduces clinical events in TRT-induced erythrocytosis, and a credible mechanistic case that aggressive/periodic phlebotomy may *increase* thrombotic risk (iron depletion → HIF stabilisation). Dose reduction + watchful waiting beats routine phlebotomy.
2. **The oxygen-transport argument is mostly a rationalisation.** Physiology says oxygen *delivery* peaks at Hct ~0.40–0.45; above ~0.45 the viscosity penalty cancels the extra carrying capacity. At 0.51 you get **essentially no oxygen-delivery benefit over 0.45 — only viscosity cost.** BUT (next point) that cost is also modest at 0.51.
3. **At 0.51 you're in a genuine grey zone, not a danger zone.** Both the O2 *benefit* and the thrombosis *harm* are small/uncertain at this level. The 0.54 guideline threshold is largely arbitrary (borrowed from polycythemia vera).
4. **For your epistaxis specifically, the evidenced lever is BLOOD PRESSURE, not haematocrit.** HTN is a strong, dose-graded epistaxis risk factor; a direct polycythemia→epistaxis link is *not* well evidenced. So candesartan + CPAP (BP control) is the evidence-based anti-bleed strategy — lowering Hct is not.
5. **CPAP will help the apnea/BP/bleeds but won't move your Hct much (~1 point).** Your Hct is TRT-driven, not OSA-driven. If you actually wanted to lower Hct, the lever is testosterone dose, not CPAP.

**Net:** the *plan* (defer donation, trial CPAP + Z2, re-measure) is sound. The *reasoning* needs correcting: you're not deferring because high Hct is beneficial (it isn't, above ~0.45) — you're deferring because the harm at 0.51 is modest, phlebotomy has its own downsides, and BP (not Hct) is what's driving the bleeds.

---

## 1. Thrombosis risk: secondary (TRT) erythrocytosis vs polycythemia vera

**Evidence grade: low–moderate, observational, conflicting. Risk is real but modest and far below PV.**

- The thrombosis concern is **largely extrapolated from polycythemia vera (PV)** — a clonal/JAK2 marrow disease where thrombosis risk runs **2.7–13.1× controls**. TRT erythrocytosis is medicament-induced with *suppressed* EPO; mechanistically different.
- **TRAVERSE** (5,246 men): VTE hazard ratio **1.46 (95% CI 0.92–2.32) — non-significant.**
- A large retrospective cohort (~74M patients): MACE + VTE elevated at **Hct ≥52% (OR 1.35, 95% CI 1.13–1.61)** — but **at a 50% cutoff, no significant difference.**
- Copenhagen General Population Study: **no significant VTE association** even at the 95th–100th percentile of Hct (HR 1.26, CI 0.92–1.72).
- Verdict: a modest signal that appears around **~52%**, not at 50%. You're at 51%.

## 2. Best trial evidence — TRAVERSE (NEJM 2023)

**Evidence grade: high (large RCT), but secondary endpoints hypothesis-generating.**

- **MACE: non-inferior** to placebo. TRT did *not* raise major cardiac events.
- **But higher rates of:** atrial fibrillation (~3.5% vs 2.2%), acute kidney injury (~2.3% vs 1.5%), **pulmonary embolism (~0.9% vs 0.5%).**
- **Crucial for the Hct question:** time-dependent Cox models found **NO association between change in haematocrit and MACE or VTE.** → Even where TRT showed a clot signal (PE/AF), **haematocrit was not the mediator.** The PE/AF signal looks like a direct testosterone (or other) effect, not a viscosity effect.

## 3. The 0.54 threshold — how arbitrary?

**Evidence grade: very low; expert consensus, not empirical derivation.**

- Guideline cutoffs (50–54%) appear **"arbitrarily chosen"** and **borrowed from PV management** without validation in secondary erythrocytosis.
- The only signal in the data sits around **≥52%** (OR 1.35); below that, nothing significant. So if a real threshold exists, it's ~52%, and 0.54 is conservative padding.

## 4. The oxygen-delivery optimum — the crux of the argument

**Evidence grade: strong (consistent physiology + haemorheology).**

- Oxygen *delivery* DO₂ = cardiac output × oxygen content. Raising Hct raises content **but also raises viscosity, which lowers cardiac output/flow.** DO₂ ∝ Hct ÷ viscosity.
- **Optimal Hct for oxygen delivery is ~0.40–0.45.** Above ~0.45, *"blood viscosity limits cardiac output such that there is little beneficial effect from the additional oxygen-carrying capacity."*
- Transfusion study: raising Hct **does not increase DO₂** unless you're severely anaemic (>60% Hct deficit); at moderate levels it can *decrease* microcirculatory oxygen delivery.
- **Reconciling altitude/doping:** endurance athletes who blood-dope raise Hct *modestly from a low-normal baseline* toward ~0.45–0.50 and gain mostly via increased total haemoglobin *mass* + other adaptations — sports science still recognises a viscosity penalty at high Hct. It is **not** evidence that 0.51 delivers more tissue O₂ than 0.45.
- **Verdict on your argument: not valid as stated.** At 0.51 you have ~zero oxygen-delivery advantage over 0.45, only viscosity cost. *However*, the viscosity cost at 0.51 is itself modest — the steep harm is higher up — so this corrects the rationale without making 0.51 dangerous.

## 5. Hyperviscosity symptoms

**Evidence grade: moderate (clinical convention).** Symptomatic hyperviscosity (headache, visual changes, etc.) generally appears at Hct **well above your level — typically >0.55–0.60**, and is uncommon below that. Consistent with you being asymptomatic at 0.51. *(Lower confidence — not deeply sourced here.)*

## 6. Epistaxis link — the most decision-relevant finding

**Evidence grade: HTN→epistaxis moderate–strong; Hct→epistaxis weak/absent.**

- **Hypertension is one of the strongest epistaxis risk factors** — highest association in multiple series (~43%), with a **progressive relationship between HTN stage and epistaxis risk/severity.**
- A **direct polycythemia/viscosity → epistaxis link is *not* well evidenced** — the literature didn't support it.
- **Implication:** for your bleeds, **blood pressure is the evidenced lever, not haematocrit.** This *supports* deferring Hct intervention and prioritising BP control (candesartan + CPAP). It also corrects earlier framing that leaned on viscosity for the bleeds — the stronger driver is BP + local vessel fragility, not Hct.

## 7. Comorbidity interactions

- **OSA** independently raises thrombotic/CV risk (and modestly raises Hct), but OSA-driven polycythemia is uncommon (prevalence ~2% overall, ~6% in severe OSA).
- **HTN** is the big evidenced epistaxis driver.
- **Tadalafil (PDE5i)** — mild vasodilator, slight bleeding-lean but also vasoprotective; not a major thrombotic factor.
- Net: your thrombotic risk is driven more by the **OSA + HTN stack** than by Hct per se — which is exactly what CPAP + the ARB address.

## 8. Management: does phlebotomy actually help?

**Evidence grade: none for benefit; mechanistic concern for harm.**

- *"Evidence supporting the efficacy or safety of therapeutic phlebotomy in lowering haematocrit in TTh-induced erythrocytosis is lacking."* No RCTs vs dose reduction.
- **Phlebotomy may paradoxically raise thrombotic risk:** iron depletion → reduced prolyl-hydroxylase → **HIF-α accumulation** → upregulated tissue factor/thrombospondin (the Chuvash erythrocytosis model: phlebotomy *increases* thrombosis, so it's specifically *not* advised there).
- **Preferred order:** (1) **testosterone dose reduction** + target comorbidities first; (2) **one-or-two limited phlebotomies** to speed correction = "unlikely to cause harm"; (3) **periodic/maintenance phlebotomy to suppress Hct = potentially harmful.**
- So: a *single* reset donation if Hct climbs is benign; *routine* donation as a lifestyle is not clearly beneficial and may carry its own risk.

## 9. CPAP effect on haematocrit

**Evidence grade: moderate (meta-analysis, single-arm trials).**

- CPAP reduces **Hct by ~1.1% (95% CI −1.4 to −0.9)** and **Hb by ~3.76 g/L (~0.4 g/dL)** — **modest.** Longer treatment → *smaller* reduction (some rebound).
- OSA-polycythemia prevalence is low (2–6%), i.e. **your 0.51 is much more likely TRT-driven than OSA-driven.**
- **Implication:** CPAP is essential for the apnea, BP, and surge-driven bleeds — but expect it to move your Hct by **~1 point at most** (0.51 → ~0.50). Don't bank on CPAP "fixing" the Hct.

## 10. Zone 2 / endurance effect on haematocrit

**Evidence grade: moderate.**

- Endurance training expands **plasma volume 9–25% (300–700 mL)** within days, lowering *measured* Hct/Hb by dilution ("sports anaemia" — not pathologic).
- **But if training continues, red-cell mass catches up over weeks** and Hct partly normalises. So the dilutional drop is real for viscosity but partly transient for the number; total Hb mass isn't removed.
- **Implication:** Z2 will lower your *measured* Hct somewhat (genuinely reduces viscosity via dilution) but won't remove red-cell mass the way phlebotomy does.

---

## What this means for the plan

- **Keep the deferral.** Defer donation, run ~3 months of CPAP + Z2, re-measure. Evidence-consistent.
- **Re-frame *why*:** not "high Hct is good for O₂" (it isn't above ~0.45) — but "harm at 0.51 is modest, phlebotomy has its own risks, and BP not Hct drives the bleeds."
- **The real anti-epistaxis levers are BP control + local vessel care** (candesartan, CPAP, saline gel, trigger avoidance), *not* lowering Hct.
- **If you do want to lower Hct, the lever is TRT dose, not CPAP** (CPAP moves it ~1 point).
- **Action threshold:** if Hct climbs to/past ~0.54 — or the modest ~52% signal worries you — go **dose reduction first, single limited phlebotomy second; avoid routine/maintenance phlebotomy.**
- **Re-measure properly:** hydrated, consistent draw time — a dehydrated morning draw inflates Hct and could fake a rise.
