# Fertility, conception, pregnancy and delivery — what the diagnosis does and doesn't change

Written 19 Aug 2026 against: MRI (conus L4, 121 mm cauda equina lipoma, S1 lesion "suggestive of dermal sinus", left L5/S1 roots), urodynamics Oct 2025 (detrusor overactivity, capacity 300 ml, residual 300 ml), lifelong bowel/bladder/sexual triad, and the fact she **never took the mirabegron (Betmiga)** — so there is no drug exposure question. Not medical advice; the people who own these decisions are the neurosurgeon, then an obstetrician + obstetric anaesthetist + (neuro-)urologist when pregnancy is on the table.

## 1. Fertility itself — expected impact: none

- Fertility is ovaries (eggs, ovulation), hypothalamus/pituitary (hormones), tubes, uterus, implantation. **None of that is wired through the sacral cord or the cauda equina.** A tethered cord/lipoma is a problem of the lowest spinal nerves — bladder, bowel, genital sensation, foot muscles. It does not touch the reproductive endocrine axis or the gametes.
- **Precedent:** women with far more severe lesions — open spina bifida (myelomeningocele) and complete spinal cord injury — have ovulation and conception rates essentially the same as the general population; menstruation is normal, they conceive naturally, they carry pregnancies. Where subfertility appears in those groups it tracks associated problems (obesity, shunted hydrocephalus, precocious puberty in childhood-onset hydrocephalus) that she doesn't have. Closed dysraphism in an otherwise healthy 29-year-old sits below those groups in severity.
- The urodynamics letter notes a **retroverted uterus** — a normal variant in ~1 in 5 women, not a fertility factor.
- Detethering surgery does not involve any reproductive organ and has no fertility effect.

**So if the question is "can she get pregnant?" — the diagnosis gives no reason to expect a problem.** Everything below is about *getting there* and *carrying/delivering safely*, which is where the diagnosis does matter.

## 2. Conception mechanics — the sexual-function side (real, workable)

- The sacral roots (S2–S4) carry genital sensation, arousal/lubrication and orgasm; the pelvic floor dysfunction adds dyspareunia risk. Her documented sexual symptoms are the same lesion. This affects **sex**, not **fertility**: conception needs sperm in the vagina around ovulation, and that is achievable with reduced sensation/arousal (lubricant, timing, patience; treating the pelvic floor). If intercourse became genuinely impossible, timed intra-uterine insemination is a simple bypass — but nothing in her picture suggests that.
- Fixing the tether may improve, stabilise, or not change genital sensation — the literature on sexual outcomes after adult detethering is thin and mixed; don't promise improvement.

## 3. Pregnancy — where the diagnosis actually bites

This is the section that matters. Ordered by weight.

### a. Anaesthesia for labour/delivery — the big one
- Epidurals and spinals are placed at L3/4 or L4/5 **on the assumption that the cord ends at L1–2.** Her cord ends at **L4** and the canal from L4/5 to the sacrum is filled by lipoma; a needle at the usual level can go into cord or lipoma. A dermal sinus (if it communicates) is also an infection route into the CSF. **A routine labour epidural is off the table without specialist planning.** In practice: an obstetric anaesthetic consult early in pregnancy with the MRI in hand; options are a higher-level epidural placed with imaging knowledge (some centres do this), no neuraxial block at all with alternatives for labour analgesia (remifentanil PCA, nitrous), and general anaesthesia if a caesarean is needed. After detethering the anatomy is *still* abnormal (low conus, scar) — the same caution applies for life. **This should go on her record and any hospital admission as a standing flag.**

### b. Neurogenic bladder in pregnancy
- Pregnancy raises UTI risk for everyone; a bladder that leaves 300 ml behind raises it more, and untreated bacteriuria/UTI in pregnancy is linked to pyelonephritis and preterm birth. Expect: urine screening each visit, low threshold to treat, and possibly clean intermittent self-catheterisation if residuals climb. A urologist with a neuro-urology interest should be on the team before conception, not after.
- **Drugs:** she never took mirabegron; if bladder treatment is revisited, mirabegron and most anticholinergics are avoided in pregnancy (limited safety data). Good timing to be off it; a reason not to start it while trying.

### c. Bowel — the megarectum gets worse in pregnancy
- Progesterone slows the gut, iron constipates, the uterus compresses the rectum. Someone with a lifelong megarectum and impaired rectal sensation needs a **written bowel programme before pregnancy** (osmotic laxative baseline, scheduled evacuation, fibre/fluid), not a scramble at 20 weeks. This is the same programme already in `treatment-options.md` — the diagnosis just makes it non-optional.

### d. Symptom change with pregnancy itself
- The lumbar lordosis, weight and ligament laxity of pregnancy put traction on a tethered cord. There are published cases of previously mild or undiagnosed tethered cord *declaring itself* in pregnancy — new pain, retention, weakness. That is one of the reasons surgeons prefer to detether a **symptomatic** patient before pregnancy rather than during. It is not a reason to panic; it is a reason for the sequencing below.

### e. Delivery mode
- Tethered cord/lipoma is **not** by itself an indication for caesarean; many women with dysraphism deliver vaginally. The individualised factors are: (i) she already has neurogenic sphincter/pelvic-floor dysfunction, and a vaginal delivery with obstetric anal sphincter injury on top of an already-compromised sphincter is a worse proposition than in someone with normal function; (ii) reduced perineal sensation can mean she doesn't perceive second stage normally — a management issue, not a contraindication; (iii) whether neuraxial anaesthesia is available changes the calculus for an emergency caesarean (GA vs. spinal). Reasonable obstetricians land on either side; the point is that the plan should be made deliberately, in a hospital with obstetric anaesthesia and neurosurgery on site (RPA fits), not defaulted.

## 4. Passing it on — the genetics/folate question

**What folate has and hasn't got to do with this.** Periconceptional folic acid (days 21–28 post-conception, when the neural tube closes) cuts *open* NTDs (spina bifida aperta, anencephaly) by ~70 % — proven in RCTs (MRC Vitamin Study 1991) and the basis for flour fortification. Her lesion is a *closed* dysraphism (lipoma + tethered cord), from a fault in secondary neurulation / skin–neural disjunction at weeks 4–6 — a different step. Post-fortification data show open NTDs fell and closed lesions largely didn't. **So folate almost certainly played no part in her having it, and no supplement her mother could have taken would reliably have prevented it.** The 5 mg advice below is applied to her by the "personal history of any NTD" rule (shared familial susceptibility across open/closed lesions), and protects mainly against an *open* NTD in her child; effect on lipoma-type lesions is unknown. Cheap, safe, worth doing; not treatment for her.

- Closed spinal dysraphism (lipomyelocele / spinal lipoma) belongs to the neural-tube-defect family. **A parent with an NTD raises the child's risk of an NTD** — the usual figure quoted for open NTDs is roughly 2–5 % vs ~0.1 % baseline; for closed lesions like hers the recurrence data are sparser and the risk is thought lower and less folate-sensitive, but the standard advice is applied to her anyway:
- **High-dose folic acid — 5 mg daily (not the standard 0.4–0.5 mg) — from at least one month before conception through the first trimester.** That is the RANZCOG/Australian recommendation for anyone with a personal or partner history of NTD. It's a prescription item; the GP can write it now.
- **Prenatal screening** for the pregnancy: the routine 18–20-week morphology scan already looks at the fetal spine; ask for a detailed fetal spine assessment. First-trimester screening and maternal serum AFP are for open lesions; closed ones can be subtle — an experienced sonographer matters.

## 5. Sequencing — the practical answer

1. **Neurosurgical opinion first** (Dr Brennan). If detethering + sinus excision is offered, do it **before** trying to conceive; allow the recovery interval he recommends (commonly ~6–12 months) before pregnancy.
2. In parallel: **5 mg folate script**, **bowel programme written down**, **neuro-urology referral** for a bladder plan (residual monitoring; whether CISC will be needed).
3. When pregnant: early booking at a tertiary hospital, **obstetric anaesthesia review in the first trimester** with the MRI, urine surveillance every visit, delivery plan discussed by 28–32 weeks. Alert on every record: *"tethered cord — conus at L4, spinal lipoma L4/5–sacrum, dermal sinus S1: no neuraxial block without anaesthetic consultant review."*
4. Shane's side (TRT/hCG plan in `~/personal/health-appearance/TRT-Health/`) is the other half of the couple's fertility and unaffected by any of this — the timing of coming off/onto hCG can be planned around her surgical timeline.

## 6. One-line summary

**Her ability to conceive is not expected to be affected. What is affected is the pregnancy and delivery plan — chiefly anaesthesia (low cord = no routine epidural), bladder infections, bowel management, and a small hereditary risk that high-dose folate addresses. Fix the tether first, then plan the pregnancy with the right team, and none of these is a barrier.**
