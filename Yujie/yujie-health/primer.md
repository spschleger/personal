## 1. The anatomy you need

- **Spinal cord** runs from the brain down the canal, but in adults it *stops* around **L1–L2** — the top of the lumbar spine. Below that, the canal contains only a bundle of nerve roots hanging down (the **cauda equina**, "horse's tail") floating in spinal fluid, plus a thin fibrous thread — the **filum terminale** — that anchors the tip of the cord to the tailbone.
- All of it sits inside a fluid-filled bag, the **thecal (dural) sac**, which normally ends around **S2** in the sacrum.
- **Why the cord ends high:** in the embryo the cord runs the full length of the spine, but the bones grow faster than the cord, so the cord "ascends" — the tip ends up at L1–2 by birth. **If the tip is stuck to something at the bottom, it can't ascend.** It gets stretched, and the lowest segments — the ones that run the bowel, bladder, genitals, and the muscles of the foot — get chronically strained. That's a **tethered cord**.
- The bottom segments (S2–S5) also happen to be exactly the nerves for: rectal sensation and the anal sphincter, bladder filling sensation and emptying, genital sensation, and the small muscles inside the foot. That is the whole symptom list.

## 3. The two explanations

**A. Tethered cord (closed spinal dysraphism)** — the cord never ascended; its tip is stuck low (here, around L5–S1), possibly by a fatty lump (a lipoma) or a thickened filum. It's congenital: it was there at birth. Typical adult story: lifelong constipation and bladder problems that everyone treated as "just her"; one leg that has always been colder, thinner, shorter; a high-arched foot with clawed toes on one side; a skin mark over the sacrum; then in the 20s–30s, pain and worsening as the tethered cord gets stretched by growth, posture, or nothing in particular. **That is her history nearly line for line — and it is why this is my leading read.** The unilateral cavus foot is the giveaway: it means the nerves to that foot's small muscles have been imbalanced since childhood, i.e. before any pain, i.e. congenital.

**B. A tumour inside the sac at L5–S1** — most likely a *myxopapillary ependymoma* (a slow, low-grade tumour that grows on the filum, classically in young adults, classically causing exactly this back/leg/sphincter pain), or a *schwannoma* (benign nerve-sheath tumour). Fits the 12-month progressive pain. Doesn't fit the childhood leg findings — a tumour that appeared in adulthood can't have made a child's foot grow into a cavus shape. Not excluded, because a mass is a mass until the contrast pictures say otherwise. Both can even coexist (ependymomas can arise in a tethered filum).

The contrast pictures decide it: enhancing lump = B; non-enhancing cord/placode continuous with the cord above = A. I have not seen those slices. The radiologist has, and the GP referred within hours — so the report answers this already; you'll read it in a few days.

## 4. What each would mean

**If A (tethered cord):**
- The treatment is a **detethering operation** — a spinal neurosurgeon opens the lower back, finds the filum/lipoma, and cuts the tether so the cord is no longer under tension. Standard, well-understood surgery; a few days in hospital; usually done in a dedicated spine/paediatric-neurosurgery-trained unit.
- What it does: **stops the progression** — the pain, the new weakness, the recent bowel change. What it doesn't reliably do: **undo damage that's already there.** Bladder/bowel function sometimes improves, often stabilises, occasionally not. Pain usually improves. The 25 years of megarectum are a downstream mechanical problem that still needs the bowel programme regardless.
- Timing: not an emergency, but a "sooner rather than later" — the neurosurgeon will want to operate before more function is lost. New saddle numbness / retention / rapid weakness = go to ED that day.
- Not everyone with a low cord gets operated on. Asymptomatic tethers are sometimes watched. **She is symptomatic and progressing**, which is exactly the group that gets offered surgery.

**If B (tumour):**
- Also surgery — resection. Myxopapillary ependymomas are low grade; complete removal is usually the cure, sometimes with radiotherapy if it can't be fully removed. Schwannomas are benign; removal is curative. Whole-spine MRI first (to check nothing else along the cord).
- Sphincter/bladder recovery is again "stabilise more than reverse."

**Either way:** the person who decides is a **spinal neurosurgeon**, the next test is likely a **whole-spine MRI** (± a repeat with better sequences), and the operation is planned, not emergency.

## 4b. How it formed — the embryology in plain terms
Around day 18–28 after conception a strip on the embryo's back folds into the neural tube (future cord); the lumbar part zips shut ~day 26–27. As it closes, the overlying skin layer is meant to **peel away cleanly (disjunction)** and the loose tissue between (mesenchyme) then flows in to form dura, the bony arch and back muscles. Two mirror-image faults in that step explain everything on her scan:
- **Premature disjunction → the lipoma.** Skin lets go *before* the tube is fully closed → mesenchyme reaches the *inside* lining of the tube → tissue that touches the inside is induced to become **fat** instead of dura/bone → a lipoma fused to the back of the cord, continuous with the fat under the skin, with the bony arch behind it deficient (a *lipomyelocele*). Her 121 mm mass from L4/5 to the coccyx.
- **Incomplete disjunction → the dermal sinus.** A thread of skin *fails* to let go → as the cord later rises and the back grows, that thread is drawn out into a skin-lined tube from a surface dimple inward. Hers at S1. The two together at neighbouring levels is a recognised pairing.
The lipoma anchors the cord's tip, so when the spine outgrows the cord (tip normally rises to L1–2 by a few months of age) hers stayed at **L4**. Chronic traction on the lowest segments — bowel, bladder, genitals, small foot muscles — over 29 years is the "syndrome"; the lipoma wrapping the left L5/S1 roots is the last year's pain.
**Cause:** sporadic in almost every case; no single gene; a weak familial tendency; the risk factors for *open* spina bifida (maternal diabetes, obesity, valproate) have little demonstrated bearing on closed lipomas; folate not implicated (see `fertility-and-pregnancy.md` §4). It happened ~3–4 weeks after conception, before pregnancy would have been known. **Nothing her mother did or didn't do.**

## 6. What to watch for now (unchanged, but this is why)

A tethered cord or a sacral mass can occasionally decompensate. Same-day ED, don't wait: **new numbness in the saddle/perineal area · new trouble starting to pass urine or retention · new or fast-worsening leg/foot weakness · loss of bowel control she doesn't feel.** These are the signs of the lowest nerves losing function acutely, and they change the surgical timeline from "planned" to "now."

## 7. Terms you'll see in the report

- **Spina bifida — is this it?** Yes, technically: the neural-tube-defect family. But hers is **spina bifida occulta / closed spinal dysraphism** (skin intact, tube closed, layers beneath separated wrongly → lipoma + tether + dermal sinus) — not **spina bifida aperta / myelomeningocele** (open lesion at birth, paralysis, hydrocephalus, Chiari, shunt). No brain involvement, silent for decades, planned adult surgery. Specialists say "closed/occult spinal dysraphism" to avoid the confusion. Family link = the "personal history of NTD" folate/scan rules only.
- **Conus (medullaris)** — the tip of the cord. "Conus at L1–2" = normal; "low-lying conus at L4/L5/S1" = tethered.
- **Filum terminale** — the anchoring thread. "Thickened / fatty filum (>2 mm)" = tether.
- **Lipoma / lipomyelocele / lipomyelomeningocele** — fat attached to the cord end, sometimes passing out through a gap in the bone to the fat under the skin. Family of tethered cord.
- **Placode** — the flattened bottom end of a tethered cord.
- **Spina bifida occulta / posterior arch defect** — a gap in the back of the vertebrae; harmless alone, meaningful with the above.
- **Dermal sinus** — a tract from a skin dimple inward toward the sac.
- **Syrinx** — a fluid cavity inside the cord, sometimes seen with tethering; a reason for whole-spine MRI.
- **Intradural extramedullary** — "inside the sac, outside the cord" — where ependymomas/schwannomas sit.
- **Enhancement** — lights up with contrast → vascular tissue → tumour more likely.
- **Tarlov cyst** — a fluid pocket on a sacral nerve root; was on our list; not what this looks like.
- **Detethering** — the operation.
